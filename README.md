# Nightroll

**A disposable camera that develops into an edit.**

One roll per night. Everyone there shoots into it. Nobody sees anything until
9am the next morning — when it develops for the whole group at once, already
cut into a finished 60-second recap.

Working name. Rename freely.

---

## The one-sentence version

Dispo, but video, and the thing that develops isn't a pile of clips — it's an
edit nobody had to make.

---

## Why this idea, and why now

### Dispo proved the appetite and then lost the company

Dispo launched around 2019 as David Dobrik's disposable camera app and
rebranded in 2020. Shoot, no preview, no filters, no deleting; photos develop
at 9am the next morning. It was genuinely hot in early 2021 — invite-only, a
long waitlist, and a reported ~$200M valuation with Spark Capital and Alexis
Ohanian's Seven Seven Six.

In March 2021 an investigation into Dobrik's Vlog Squad surfaced serious
allegations against a member of the group. He stepped off the board, both lead
investors publicly cut ties, and the company never got its momentum back.

**It died of founder scandal, not product failure.** The mechanic was working
when the floor fell out. That means the genre was *vacated*, not disproven, and
it has been sitting empty since.

### What Dispo got wrong on the product, separately from the scandal

1. **Distribution was borrowed.** The launch rode one person's audience — a
   single point of failure, and precisely how it died.
2. **The delay was a gimmick, not a mechanic.** Waiting until 9am felt novel,
   but the wait didn't *produce* anything. It only postponed.
3. **Shared Rolls were the real product and arrived late and half-built.**
   Several people shooting into one roll was the thing. It was shipped as a
   feature instead of the center.
4. **No second visit.** You checked your photos once and closed the app until
   the next party.
5. **It was a photo app for a video-native generation.**

## What "done right" means

Make the shared roll the entire product, and make it video.

### The mechanic

One roll per night. Everyone present shoots clips into it. Nobody sees anything
all night. At 9am it develops **for everyone simultaneously** — which turns the
delay from a gimmick into a synchronized social event. The group chat lights up
at the same moment because everyone is seeing it for the first time together.

That simultaneity is what Dispo left on the table. The wait should *create* a
shared moment, not merely defer a private one.

### The part that is actually hard, and actually ours

Nobody watches 47 shaky vertical clips from a night out. Everyone watches a
good 60 seconds.

Overnight, the pipeline cuts the roll into a real edit — framed, cropped to
vertical, following whoever the subject of each shot is, dead footage dropped.
You wake up to a finished recap of your night that no one made.

This is the whole moat. A shared album is a commodity; several large companies
already ship one. **An automatic edit that is genuinely good enough to post is
not**, and it is the specific thing this pipeline already does: track subjects
across frames, rank who matters, crop to follow them.

Everyone else's version of this idea ends at 47 clips in a shared folder.

### Keep every constraint

No preview. No deleting. No filters. Limited clips.

The constraint is why you get the real night instead of a curated one, and it
is the only thing separating this from a shared album with extra steps.

### Falls out for free

Per-person cuts. Same engine — everyone pulls their own version of the night,
following them.

## The real problem: cold start

Not all network effects are equally hard. This one has close to the worst
possible shape, and it deserves to be stated plainly rather than hand-waved.

### Why it is unusually severe

- **Co-presence, not just co-adoption.** A messaging app works if your friend
  has it anywhere on earth. This needs your friends to have it *and* be in the
  same room *and* on the same night. Spatial, temporal and social coincidence
  at once.
- **It is a step function, not a gradient.** Most network products give
  incremental value per friend added. Here value is roughly zero until ~5
  people at one event have it, then high. You cannot climb to the threshold —
  you have to jump it.
- **A bad first night is fatal.** Two of eight people shooting produces a thin,
  disappointing roll. The entire promise is "you wake up to something great."
  One mediocre morning and that friend group is gone permanently. The failure
  mode is a broken promise, not indifference.
- **Trial is occasion-gated.** You cannot evaluate this on a Tuesday
  afternoon. You wait for a night out, so the learning loop is a week long.
- **The payoff is 12 hours after install.** The longest possible gap between
  acquisition and the aha moment. The magic cannot be demoed at the door.

### The reframe

**Stop trying to acquire users. The unit of adoption is a night.**

Every mechanic should be designed to convert an entire room at once. That one
shift reorders everything below it.

### The plan, in order of leverage

**1. Nobody installs anything at the party.**

The big one. One person starts the roll; everyone else joins by QR code
straight into a browser camera. No app store, no account, no signup.

At a party — half-drunk, bad wifi, "I'm not downloading an app" — the install
ask is where you lose the room. It is exactly where Dispo put it.

Then flip the install to the moment of maximum motivation instead of maximum
friction: next morning the text says *your night is ready*, and opening it
requires the app. Same install, entirely different conversion rate, because now
they are installing to collect something they already know exists and want.

**2. The morning edit is the acquisition channel.**

It is a video, it gets posted, and it lands in front of exactly the right
demographic with a built-in "how did you make that." Every night out becomes a
distribution event.

This only works if the edit is good enough to post unprompted — which is again
the pipeline, and again the reason nobody else can run this play.

**3. Seed by scene, not by city.**

Not "launch in Austin." Launch inside one dense graph with *repeated*
co-presence: a single Greek system, a dorm floor, a run club, one music scene.

You need the same people in the same rooms week after week so the threshold is
crossed repeatedly rather than once. Density beats reach. This is the real
reason the one-campus play works — not because campuses are fashionable.

**4. Go through organizers, not attendees.**

Weddings, formals, birthdays, festivals, spring break houses, graduation. Each
has one person already coordinating everybody and already wanting a recap
video. One conversation converts thirty people at once.

Weddings are the sharpest wedge: someone is already paying thousands of dollars
for precisely this output.

**5. Never let a roll come out bad.**

Make it degrade gracefully. Allow clips imported from the camera roll after the
fact with the edit re-rendering, so a thin night can be rescued and latecomers
can still contribute. Enforce a quality floor before the morning message is
allowed to send at all.

Protecting the first experience matters more than any growth tactic, because
the first experience **is** the growth tactic.

## The test that tells you it is working

Two things, both cheap to measure:

1. Does a group that used it once use it again the next weekend, unprompted?
2. Did anyone join a roll because they saw someone's edit?

If both are yes, the loop is closed and the rest is execution.

If the second is no, the edit is not good enough yet — and no amount of seeding
will save it.

---

## Open questions

- **Copyright / content:** minimal here, unlike the fan-edit idea. Mostly clean.
- **Privacy:** everyone in a roll is filmed by other people. Who can pull the
  roll, who can remove themselves, what happens to footage of someone who
  leaves the group. Needs an answer before launch, not after.
- **Cost:** rendering an edit per roll per night. Needs a unit-economics pass
  once the pipeline shape is known.
- **Where the pipeline runs:** on-device versus server changes the privacy
  story and the cost story simultaneously.

## Appendix: adjacent ideas on the same engine

All three share the same core — track subjects across frames, decide who
matters, crop to follow them. They are worth listing together because building
any one of them substantially builds the others.

### Camera Roll Wrapped *(parked, but the strongest standalone)*

Point the pipeline at a year of someone's camera roll. Who is actually in your
life: top five people by seconds on camera, who you filmed in January versus
December, who you film but who never films you, the month you vanished.

Then auto-generate a vertical recap edit per person. The shareable object is a
video, not a stat card — which is the part only this pipeline makes possible.

Wrapped-shaped, identity-artifact, emotionally loaded, and nobody has done it
for the camera roll because until recently nobody could.

### Fancam Machine

Stan culture runs on fancams and edits: a vertical clip following exactly one
person through a scene. Currently made by hand, frame by frame, by teenagers
with more patience than software.

Drop in any footage, tap a face, get a vertical cut that follows only them.

The output is posted publicly by default with people asking how it was made,
and it makes an unskilled person look talented — the best thing a tool can do.
Lives in the same copyright gray zone the whole edit scene does.

### Cropped Out

Upload a group video; it finds the person the frame keeps leaving behind and
makes *their* cut instead. Pure comedy, one afternoon of work, good top-of-funnel
for any of the above.

## Prototype: `nightroll.py`

Builds the edit **twice** from the same folder of clips — same selection, same
ordering, same pacing, same music — changing only the framing:

- `nightroll_naive.mp4` — centre-crop to 9:16. **The control.**
- `nightroll_piped.mp4` — each clip through the smart-cropping pipeline. **The treatment.**

Watch them back to back. That comparison is the whole experiment: if the
difference is not obvious, the concept does not work, and you found out in a
week instead of after building an app.

### Run it

```bash
# Fast sanity check. No pipeline, no GPU -- just probes and prints the plan.
python nightroll.py ~/footage/saturday --dry-run

# The real thing.
python nightroll.py ~/footage/saturday --out ./out --music track.m4a

# Pace to a tempo instead of a fixed slot (4 beats per clip).
python nightroll.py ~/footage/saturday --bpm 120 --music track.m4a
```

Needs `ffmpeg`/`ffprobe` on PATH. Nothing else outside the standard library.
The pipeline half shells out to `run_pipeline_local.py` in a smart-cropping
checkout — override with `--repo`, `--env`, `--workspace` if yours differ from
the defaults.

### What it actually does

1. **Probe** every clip: duration, dimensions, capture time, average brightness.
2. **Filter** on duration and brightness. Rejections are printed with reasons.
3. **Group** clips into events on capture-time gaps (default: a gap over 6
   hours starts a new event), because a gathered folder is rarely one night.
   It reports what it found and uses the biggest event by default.
4. **Select** by bucketing that event into equal time windows and taking the
   longest clip from each, so the edit spans the whole event rather than
   clustering wherever people filmed most.
5. **Crop** the survivors through the pipeline, one subprocess per clip. A clip
   that fails is logged and skipped, never fatal.
6. **Cut** a fixed-length window from the middle of each clip and concatenate to
   1080x1920 / 30fps, with optional music over the top.

Every run writes `plan.json`: what was chosen, what was rejected and why, and
where each artifact landed.

### Photos as well as video

A night out is mostly photos, so the tool takes both. Stills are held for the
slot length with a slow push (a photo sitting dead still inside a music-cut
edit reads as a broken video; a gentle zoom reads as intentional), and they
interleave with video clips in capture order.

Photos test the framing question too, just a different half of it: a group
photo centre-cropped to 9:16 chops people out of frame, and that is a very
visible failure for subject-aware cropping to beat. Video is the harder case
because the crop has to track motion, so within a time bucket a video outranks
a photo.

`.jpg`, `.jpeg`, `.png`, `.webp`, `.heic` and `.heif` are accepted.

#### HEIC needs two passes, and lies about its size

Worth knowing because both failures are silent rather than loud:

- An iPhone HEIC is **tiled HEVC**, and ffmpeg stitches the tiles with an
  internal complex filtergraph. Adding a simple `-vf` on top is rejected
  outright, so HEICs are decoded to JPEG first and filtered after.
- `ffprobe` reports the size of the **first tile** (typically 512x512), not
  the image. Spotlight has the real dimensions, so HEIC sizes come from
  `mdls` instead.

### Footage from more than one night

Fine — that is the normal case, and the tool expects it.

```bash
python nightroll.py ~/footage --dry-run          # see what events it found
python nightroll.py ~/footage --event 3          # build from event 3
python nightroll.py ~/footage --gap-hours 12     # coarser grouping
python nightroll.py ~/footage --pool             # ignore events entirely
```

For the framing experiment specifically, **coherence does not matter**.
`--pool` is the right call: the question is whether automatic framing beats a
centre-crop on bad amateur footage, and a reel stitched from four different
nights answers that just as well as one night would. Do not let "I need a
proper single night" hold up the test.

It matters for the *second* question — whether the output feels like a night
worth posting — and that one is worth answering later, on real single-event
footage, once framing has earned it.

#### One thing to get right while gathering

**Copy with `cp -p`, or AirDrop rather than re-export.** Plain copying rewrites
mtime, and many phone exports carry no `creation_time` tag at all, so every
clip lands with the same timestamp. That silently destroys both event grouping
and running order.

The tool detects this and says so — across the whole folder, and within a
single event when only that batch was flattened — but it can only warn. It
cannot recover a timestamp that is gone.

### What is deliberately faked

Named honestly, because they are all cheap to fix later and none of them are
what this test is asking:

- **Pacing** — a fixed slot, or `4 * 60 / bpm`. No beat detection.
- **Window** — the middle of the clip. People record before anything happens
  and stop after it stops; the middle is a guess, not a content decision.
- **Coverage** — spread over *time within one event*, not over *people*. Per-person coverage
  needs cross-clip face re-identification, which is real work and would be the
  first thing to build once framing is proven.
- **Quality** — duration and brightness only. No shake detection.

### The trap

Do not test this on `white_lotus_2`, `friends_rachel_and_ross`, or
`big_bang_theory`. They are professionally shot, lit and composed, they are
sitting right there in the workspace, and they will give you a false positive.

The product lives or dies on amateur footage: mixed orientation, shaky,
backlit, a thumb over the lens, filmed at 1am. You need 30-50 clips from one
real event across several different phones. That is the only input that
answers the question.
